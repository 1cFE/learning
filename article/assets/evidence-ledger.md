# Compact-learning evidence ledger

Review date: 30 September 2026. The illustration compares supported and insufficiently supported deployment paths for 10 MWe compact and 1 GWe large designs. It connects production learning, wholesale competitiveness, subsequent growth, the conditions for $10/MWh, and the cumulative premium paid by early customers or funders. Every model input is a chosen assumption. Source type and publication status remain separate from a claim's evidence grade.

## Chosen trajectory assumptions

The learning illustration does not specify a fuel or confinement method. Its inputs are assumed electricity costs, net unit output, completed production, delivery times and customer support. It estimates no named company's product or market.

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Net unit output | 10 MWe | 1 GWe | Chosen design scales |
| FOAK electricity cost | $500/MWh | $250/MWh | Hypothetical LCOEs in constant 2025 US dollars, not project capital |
| Common fixed contribution | $5/MWh | $5/MWh | Aggressive chosen budget for costs not reduced by the modeled learning, not a measured engineering minimum |
| Learning above the floor | 25%; additional sensitivities at 10%, 20% and 30% | Main 25% and 10% sensitivity | Whole-unit experience is a simplifying accounting boundary |

| Annual delivery ceiling | 2,500 units, 25 GWe | 25 reactors, 25 GWe | Chosen common industrial scale limit |
| Insufficient support | Stops after 31 units total | Seven units total over twenty years | Economic failure cases with limited support for learning |
| Wholesale reference | $60/MWh | $60/MWh | Hypothetical comparison, not a current market quotation |
| Operating life and capacity factor | 30 years, 90% | Same | Chosen premium-accounting assumptions, not demonstrated performance |
| Observation horizon | 45 years | 45 years | Tests continued production to $10/MWh; distinct from the 30-year unit life |

Costs are fixed at order start using completed experience. The [assumptions](./assumptions.json) and [model note](./model-note.md) specify the full schedules. No learning is credited to units under construction or to other units in the same cohort.

The primary cumulative premium is the undiscounted full-life payment gap above $60/MWh for all cohorts commissioned through year forty-five. It equals the sum of positive cohort cost gaps multiplied by cohort capacity and assumed lifetime generation. Above-wholesale pipeline commitments count even when a later new order has become competitive. Below-wholesale cohorts contribute zero rather than a negative credit. A secondary measure records payments only during the forty-five-year plotted period.

The main lifetime totals are $23.146 billion for supported compact, $12.712 billion for compact support ending, $225.279 billion for limited large, and $1,314.177 billion for supported large. These are modeled revenue gaps that customers, subsidies or both could cover. They are not estimates of public expenditure, upfront funding, construction capital or development cost.

Supported compact crosses wholesale for new orders in year nine after 237 units and carries 2.37 GW of above-wholesale commitments. Supported large crosses in year twelve after 37 reactors, but another 39 above-wholesale reactors are already ordered. Its premium therefore covers 76 GW. Mathematical wholesale thresholds are 200 compact units and 37 large reactors. Discrete cohorts and the delivery pipeline explain the larger scheduled commitments.

Under the main assumptions, the compact $10/MWh threshold is 64,317 completed units, or 643.17 GW and 15.97 doublings. The schedule reaches 64,514 units in year forty-one, giving a new-order cost of $9.994/MWh and first qualifying delivery in year forty-two. At year forty-five its new-order cost is $9.70/MWh, compared with $20.37/MWh for supported large. The fixed $5/MWh contribution and sustained 25% learning are conditions required by this illustration. No bottom-up design validates them.

The [target envelope](./target-envelope.csv) varies the learning rate or fixed contribution for compact while retaining the order rules and 45-year observation horizon. At a fixed $5/MWh contribution, a 20% rate requires at least 1,581,282 units for $10/MWh, while a 30% rate requires 7,555. At 25% learning, a $2/MWh contribution requires 21,030 units and an $8/MWh contribution requires 576,460. These are mathematical minima, not forecasts or optimized procurement requirements. A fixed contribution at or above $10/MWh rules out finite-volume achievement under the model.

Fewer past doublings do not create additional future learning potential at the same current cost and common floor. Each design moves from $60/MWh to $46.25/MWh with one more completed-production doubling. At the calendar date when large crosses, compact is already cheaper, so large has more absolute cost left above the floor. Subsequent reductions still depend on future production.

The calculation grants customers, finance, suppliers, delivery capacity and service. It does not validate those conditions, market saturation, capital requirements or bankability. Immediate transfer of completed-unit experience is also assumed. Rework, redesign, supplier learning, retirement and physical scale-up do not supply separate numerical cost reductions. Completed experience persists after a unit retires, while that unit stops generating electricity and premium payments. Cumulative completed GW is not the surviving operating fleet.

## Timing and sizing claims

Factory production lead time, order-to-service lead time, throughput, rework outage and design revision cycle are defined separately in the article. Prototype build records are not used to establish any of the chosen commercial schedule inputs.

A 10 MWe mine or military load is an illustrative customer requirement supplied for the scenario, not a claimed typical load or measured market size. A 100 MWe product can be too large for that example. The corridor allows larger individual reactors as later products, separately from adding more unchanged modules. No cost reduction or perfect learning transfer is assigned automatically to that scale-up.

Alaska's reported annual sales remain a factual example of why annual energy, geographical distribution and product size must be distinguished. The roughly 60 MW energy-equivalent total is spread over 188 communities and does not establish six suitable 10 MWe customers. It does not set a numerical order limit in the hypothetical comparison.

## Scope of cost comparisons

Cost comparisons require matched whole-plant boundaries, net output, installation scale, utilization, operating life, replacement costs and financing assumptions. The present trajectories use hypothetical electricity prices and production experience. They do not provide a design-specific capital-cost estimate or a numerical ranking against another corridor's reactor design.

## Whole-plant cost budget

The article derives an annual cost allowance from the hypothetical output, utilization and target price: 10 MWe × 8,760 hours/year × 90% = 78,840 MWh/year; at $10/MWh this allows $788,400/year including capital recovery. Each $5/MWh contribution corresponds to $394,200/year. These are arithmetic cost constraints, not component estimates or a demonstrated reactor operating point.
